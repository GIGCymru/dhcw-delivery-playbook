# Our values and ways of working

This playbook is mostly process: phases, roles, ceremonies, engineering
standards. However, our processes should only exist as an expression of our
values and mission, so this section looks at the core principles we stand on as
an organisation. Everything later in the playbook rests on them.

We also understand that practice varies across DHCW teams today, and some of what
follows describes where we're going more than where we are. What's here has been
built up over time, from lessons learned by the teams behind our most visible
products and services.

## Our mission

DHCW is here to make digital a force for good in health and care. We work in
partnership to provide the digital and data products and services that health and
social care in Wales depend on, and we run or work with more than 100 of them.

Shipping software is how we get there, never the point in itself. Force for good
is a test we can apply to our own work, because it asks who benefits and how we'd
know. A product or service that saves the organisation money and costs a district
nurse twenty minutes a day hasn't passed it.

[The Well-being of Future Generations (Wales) Act 2015](https://futuregenerations.wales/discover/about-future-generations-commissioner/future-generations-act-2015/) asks something similar of
every Welsh public body: meet the needs of the present without compromising the
ability of future generations to meet theirs. Accessibility and the Welsh
language sit alongside that. They're legal duties, and they apply from the first
Sprint.

## Strategic principles

These principles are part of the decision framework we use when we have to make a
trade-off. They're set out in the <a href="https://dhcw.nhs.wales/about-us/key-documents/strategies/organisational-strategy-2024-2030/our-principles/">DHCW organisational strategy 2024 to 2030</a>.
The right-hand column is this playbook's reading of what they ask of a delivery
team.

| Principle | What it asks of a delivery team |
| --- | --- |
| Put people first | We start from the needs of patients, clinicians and the public, and we test our assumptions with them before we build. |
| Simplify | We take steps out. Fewer systems, fewer handoffs, fewer things a user has to learn. |
| Design for more data, more digital | We build for reuse and interoperability, on the assumption that what we capture once will be needed somewhere else. |
| Find more value | We look for the version that costs less to run and gives more back, and we say so when something isn't worth building. |
| Learn from the past and embrace the future | We find out what's already been tried here before we start, and we're willing to change our minds about it. |

## Compassionate leadership

NHS Wales uses the compassionate leadership approach developed with Health
Education and Improvement Wales (HEIW), grounded in [Professor Michael West's research](https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/long-reads/what-is-compassionate-leadership). It
describes four behaviours, and they belong to everyone in a team, whatever their
job title.

| Behaviour | What it asks of us |
| --- | --- |
| Attending | Paying full attention to colleagues and being present with them, noticing how people are as well as what they are doing. |
| Understanding | Taking time to understand the situation people face, rather than jumping to conclusions or solutions. |
| Empathising | Feeling with people and acknowledging their experience, so that they feel heard. |
| Helping | Taking thoughtful action to remove obstacles and support people to do their best work. |

These behaviours complement good delivery practice. They line up closely with the
values set out in the [Agile Manifesto](https://agilemanifesto.org/) and the [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/).

## Psychological safety

Compassionate leadership creates the conditions for psychological safety, which
is the shared belief that it's safe to take interpersonal risks: to ask a
question, admit a mistake, challenge a decision, or raise a concern without fear
of being blamed or diminished. Creating that climate is what compassionate
leadership is for. A team that has it can talk about errors, about being
overloaded, and if they are empowered they can seek to solve these problems
themselves in the open. Everything within this playbook should be seen as
starting with a conversation.

!!! info "What psychological safety is, and is not"

    Psychological safety doesn't mean avoiding hard discussions. A common worry
    is that compassionate leadership makes tough performance conversations
    impossible, or gets them labelled as bullying. The research points the other
    way.

    Attending means paying attention to colleagues regularly, not only when
    something has gone wrong. Understanding means asking what's actually
    happening and listening to the answer before forming a view. A difficult
    conversation goes better when both of those have already happened, because
    you know the person, you know the context, and they know you've been paying
    attention.

    Safe teams are harder on the work and easier on each other. NHS research
    shows learning and improvement happen where people feel safe, and that blame
    cultures suppress both. It's why we write bugs up as information about the
    system, why delivery metrics coach the team instead of ranking it, and why we
    hold retrospectives while problems are still cheap to fix.

Safety is made by what happens after someone speaks. Whether the concern gets
looked into. Whether the person who raised it is treated differently a month
later. Whether anything actually changes. Those are management decisions before
they are team ones, and protecting them is a delivery responsibility as much as a
pastoral one.

## Agile and Scrum values

The Agile Manifesto is four short statements about where to put the emphasis. We
value both sides of each pair, and we lean towards the first.

> - Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
> - Working software over comprehensive documentation
> - Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
> - Responding to change over following a plan

Scrum names five values. Here's what they look like in our teams.

| Scrum value | How it shows up in our teams |
| --- | --- |
| Commitment | We commit to a Sprint goal together, and to helping each other reach it. |
| Focus | We work on the Sprint goal. Work that arrives mid-Sprint gets discussed before it gets absorbed. |
| Openness | We're open about the work, the progress and the problems, including with people outside the team. |
| Respect | We treat each other as capable people who can be trusted with hard things. |
| Courage | We take on the hard problems and raise concerns, including the ones where we might turn out to be wrong. |

## Working in short cycles

We plan in short cycles because we can't know everything up front. Complex work
teaches you as you go. So we show the work, look at it closely and often, and
change what we're doing when the evidence points somewhere else. Every ceremony
and every metric in this playbook is a version of that loop.

It fails in predictable ways. Work that isn't visible can't be inspected.
Standups and reviews nobody has prepared for stop telling us anything. A
retrospective that produces no change is a meeting.

## Self-organisation

We give a team a goal and the support to reach it. The team works out how.

Structure still exists. It comes from shared practices, a clear Definition of
Done, and the cadence of ceremonies. Accountability still exists too: the team
works in the open, and it makes commitments it expects to be held to. What
changes is that good work no longer has to be handed down and checked from above.

Self-organising doesn't mean unsupported. Some organisational and strategic
goals, clinical safety standards, architectural constraints and funding come from
outside the team. The goal is that a team can solve the vast majority of its
problems within the team, and empower itself to do so, not that a team sits in
isolation from context.
